Water Splash Rendering and Simulation

Created2026-11-21|Updated2026-11-22|Technology SharingGraphics Related
|Post Views:

Written before

  More than a year has passed since the previous Water Caustics. In the meantime I graduated, turned down a higher salary in pursuit of work-life balance, and joined a foreign game company. While slacking off at my desk, I came across an article on large-scale FLIP fluid simulation that produced a nice DamBreak on a consumer desktop GPU. My fingers itched, so I picked up my long-shelved research on mobile fluid splashes again, and it turned out pretty well in the end (otherwise this post would not exist).

  The scene was inspired by the Odyssey, which I have been reading lately; I quite like this kind of mythological epic. There is a practical reason too: mobile devices cannot handle desktop-level particle counts, so balancing precision against scale, I went with the shortcut of a message in a bottle. The final result runs 64K particles at a stable 100+ FPS on a high-end phone (Redmi K90pm) and 32K particles at a stable 80+ FPS on a mid-to-low-end phone (Redmi K50), which is decent performance (adding a PBD tag cord later cost a bit of performance, but… it is quite fun).

Simulation

  In GAMES201, Yuanming Hu summarizes water simulation as Lagrangian particle methods and Eulerian grid methods, each with its own limitations: particle methods (SPH, PBF) struggle with stability and incompressibility, while grid methods (LBM) are bad at splashes. So the final implementation had to be a hybrid, and after comparing several options I chose MLS-MPM, which looks good and performs stably. MLS-MPM can do much more, such as sand and snow, but multi-material interaction is still too much to hope for on mobile, so let’s just show splashes for now.

  MLS-MPM was proposed by Yuanming Hu at SIGGRAPH 2018 and is essentially an accelerated version of traditional MPM. It keeps the B-spline weights, but uses the MLS approximation to write the weight gradient directly as the weight times the node offset. This removes the separate weight gradient computation and lets the stress and the APIC affine term merge into one matrix that is scattered in a single pass. In code, the simulation splits into boundary caching at initialization, plus clearing the grid, particle-to-grid, grid update, and grid-to-particle at runtime.

Initialization

  At this scale there is no escaping GPU Compute Shaders. In Unity, C# creates the buffers, sets parameters and dispatches the work, while particle updates stay on the GPU and rendering reads the same buffer, so tens of thousands of particles never need to be copied back to the CPU each frame.

  The first step of initialization is seeding particles. Since the bottle has an irregular shape, its shape must first be extracted from the bottle mesh for initialization and later SDF lookups. I flatten it into a revolved profile, describing the inner wall with 2D points; after estimating the water volume from the circular cross sections, particles can be seeded evenly. Per particle, MLS-MPM only needs position, velocity, density ratio, and an affine matrix recording how velocity varies nearby (a concept from APIC).

  In storage, however, it is split into five contiguous arrays (SoA), which brought a nice performance gain.

Particle to Grid

  Each substep clears the grid first. The first step of P2G is computing weights: each particle distributes its mass and momentum to the surrounding 3×3×3 nodes, using a quadratic B-spline per axis; the product of the three axes gives the node weight.

  Next come stress and scattering. Water here is weakly compressible: a density ratio above 1 gets a cubic pressure pushing outward, while below 1 is ignored, so negative pressure does not suck particles into clumps. Add a bit of viscosity, combine it with the affine matrix into a matrix q, and multiplying by the node offset gives the momentum to scatter. The density ratio is the one collected for free in the previous G2P, saving whatever can be saved.

  Finally, the biggest trouble in P2G: tens of thousands of particles add to the same nodes simultaneously, which requires atomic operations, and mobile has no floating-point atomic add. My solution was to scale the values into fixed point and pack two signed 16-bit numbers into one int, so each node only needs two InterlockedAdds. The scale factors (512 for mass, 32 for momentum) were tuned between avoiding overflow and keeping precision. It is a trick, but after trying many methods it was the only optimization that worked (somehow that feels a bit sad).

Grid to Particle

  The grid update is simple: unpack mass and momentum, divide to get velocity, add gravity, then handle the bottle wall and the boat.

  Then G2P uses the same quadratic B-spline weights to gather velocity from the 27 nodes, rebuilds the affine matrix and density ratio along the way, and finally integrates the position.

  The 4 here is actually 4/Δx² for the quadratic B-spline; in grid coordinates Δx=1, leaving just 4. It is the same thing as MLS skipping the weight gradient earlier: the velocity gradient is assembled directly from weight times node offset, and the next P2G uses it as is, so particles carry local rotation and shear instead of being smoothed out by the grid every round. Meanwhile the collected density ratio is written into velocity.w for the next substep’s P2G pressure.

  As for boundaries, the grid only removes velocity pointing into the wall, which handles “don’t go in”; integrated particles may still cross it, so G2P projects them back along the bottle profile with a small clearance. Particles that touched the wall have their affine matrix cleared, otherwise the layer against the wall gets strange rotations.

Rendering

  After simulation we get a pile of points, and making it look like a continuous body of water requires reconstructing the surface. Marching Cubes is too expensive on mobile, so I went with classic screen-space fluid rendering, which only handles what the camera can see. The steps can be summarized as: particle depth + thickness → bilateral smoothing → normal reconstruction → lighting → foam composite → spray.

Screen Space

  Each particle draws a two-triangle quad, which the fragment shader cuts into a circle and computes the true sphere depth. Two depths are written here: a linear one for later smoothing and reconstruction, and a projected one for depth testing; the two must not be mixed.

  The thickness pass uses additive blending to accumulate the chord length of the view ray through each sphere, used later for light absorption. Then comes the bilateral filter: a plain blur would smear the water into the background, so besides screen distance the weight also considers depth difference, skipping samples that differ too much. Four horizontal and vertical rounds with shrinking scale aim to erase the outlines of individual particles.

  With smoothed depth, unproject back to view space, and the cross product of neighboring pixel differences gives the normal. Shading is the usual real-time approximation: exponential decay of the background color by thickness for absorption, sampling the scene color (Opaque Texture) with normal-offset UVs for refraction, and a Cubemap with Fresnel for reflection.

Foam and Spray

  The water surface was there, but no matter how hard the boat stirred it, something always felt missing. So I added a separate 8K whitewater particle pool. It does not take part in MPM; it only reads the existing grid velocity and mass, and splits into foam drifting on the surface and spray flying off.

  At emission, each free slot picks a random water particle and first checks whether it is on the surface: the gradient of the surrounding grid mass serves as the normal, and fake interfaces squeezed out by the bottle wall or the hull are excluded. Then speed plus the strain and vorticity in the affine matrix give an activity estimate, converted into a probability per time step, so the emission rate does not change with frame rate.

  Particles rushing outward fast become spray, affected only by gravity and drag; the rest become foam, sticking to the surface and drifting with the grid velocity, disappearing once they sink into the water or their life runs out. Foam is not drawn as individual white rings either: it is first laid along the surface as stretched ellipses and accumulated into a density map, overlaid with a Voronoi bubble texture, and finally composited onto the water surface by density. Only when connected into a sheet does it look like foam; otherwise it is just a pile of white-rimmed particles. Spray is drawn separately after the water composite, stretched slightly along its screen velocity, with soft edges against scene depth and water depth.

Miscellaneous

Boat-Water Coupling

  For the boat pushing water, the capsule collider on the boat is the shape source. Each frame the capsule endpoints and radius, along with five probe positions (front, back, left, right, center), are synced to the fluid in one go. The grid update forbids water from moving into the hull relative to it, and G2P pushes out particles that end up inside the capsule. Since the boat tilts, the hull velocity also needs the angular velocity contribution:

  For water carrying the boat, there is no need to read particles back to the CPU either, since the grid already holds velocity and mass. A probe is placed at the front, back, left, right and center of the boat; in nearby grid columns it searches top-down for where mass changes from air to water as the surface height, then takes the velocity a few cells below as the flow. The nodes right under the hull have already been constrained by the boat itself, and sampling them would make the boat follow itself, so they are skipped.

  Probe results are read back asynchronously every 0.1 seconds. The center decides the draft height, the front-back and left-right height differences are crossed into a surface normal for pitch and roll, and the flow velocity carries the boat along. Strictly speaking this is not buoyancy integration, just cheap bobbing with the waves, but it is enough for a small sailboat.

Tag Cord Simulation

  The tag hanging from the bottle neck is simple PBD: 16 cord segments plus the two lower corners of the tag, 19 points in total, with the tag constrained into a small rigid triangle by its three edges. Each step first applies gravity and wind (gusts built from Perlin noise, with extra wind pressure on the tag face), then iterates distance constraints, bending constraints between alternate points, and a twist constraint turning the tag toward the camera, and finally handles collisions with the table, bottle body, neck and cork, recovering velocity from the position change.

  Each frame the cord mesh sweeps an octagonal tube along the nodes using parallel transport (moving along the cord with only the minimal rotation per ring, so it does not twist like a braid). Grabbing pulls the nearest node on screen, and releasing keeps its velocity. It swings quite elastically; it costs a bit more performance, but it is genuinely fun.

Written behind

  After adding a tiny bit of detail, a message in a bottle where you can drag the boat to push water and swing the tag was finished. It fulfilled a dream from years ago and showed that 3D fluid simulation on mobile is feasible, although there is still some distance before it can enter game scenes for players to experience.

  Of course there are many shortcuts here: the boundary only supports bottles that are solids of revolution along the X axis, the boat follows kinematically rather than with real buoyancy, fast dragging has no continuous collision detection, and the transparent light path is only approximated. But as a demo it is already fun enough. The end.
